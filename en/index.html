About

Accompanying from
within, not from above

My story as a therapist begins in the aseptic isolation of a Romanian neonatal ward. Before I had words, my nervous system had already learned a pattern: how to inhabit emptiness. I grew up in a house where my father's emotional state set the weather of every room. I became a radar. I learned to read the weight of a silence, the specific vibration of a voice before it broke.

For many years I worked to appear capable and at ease in the world while inside an anxiety I rarely named hummed beneath everything. For a time, substances were the only truce with my nervous system. At thirty-one, my life is the daily practice of integration: learning to stay in my body when the old impulse to leave appears.

I come to this work as someone who has navigated the collapse of his own structures. My work begins from one conviction: we were wounded in relationship and in relationship we can heal. That is why I offer a space where mind and body work together, within a safe bond.

Areas of work

What brings
people here

I accompany the most authentic parts of ourselves that we push to the margins to keep functioning. The tension that accumulates in the body, the visceral impulse to hide, the exhaustion of holding up a fortress when the foundations are trembling.

01

Trauma+

Trauma doesn't always take the shape of a dramatic event. Sometimes it's an absence, a silence, a pattern learned before there were words. I work with the body that still reacts to something that happened long ago.

I work with trauma through the Somatosensory body model and the Polyvagal model, paying attention to how the autonomic nervous system organizes defense responses.

02

Anxiety+

I know the background hum of an anxiety that was never quite named. The radar that doesn't switch off, the permanent vigilance. We work for the nervous system to learn that the danger has passed.

I struggled with anxiety throughout my own life. I know it in the body, in patterns of relating, in the way one anticipates the world before anything has happened.

03

Depression and Burnout+

The exhaustion of holding up a façade when the foundations are trembling. The emptiness behind an outer life that works. I offer a space where that trembling is allowed to exist.

My experience in the corporate world gives me a direct understanding of burnout: performance as identity, productivity as the highest value, the collapse that arrives when the system can no longer hold.

04

Addictions+

Substances and compulsive behaviors are survival strategies — desperate attempts to find safety when the world feels too loud or too empty. I approach them with respect, not judgment.

I struggled with several addictions myself. I speak about this from knowing what it is to need an exit when the body is exhausted by another way of being.

05

Avoidant Personality+

Self-sufficiency and emotional distance as the only home one has ever known. I work specifically with those who use independence to protect themselves from the fear of being truly seen.

Avoidance is a learned response in an environment where real contact had too high a cost. The work is to slowly reduce that cost, without forcing an openness the system is not yet ready to hold.

06

Non-Dual Mindfulness+

The territory where psychology and meaning meet. For those who feel that their suffering also has an existential dimension, and that healing passes through recovering some form of connection to something larger than the self.

I have eleven years of personal practice in Buddhism and Dzogchen — one of the oldest contemplative traditions of Tibet, oriented toward the direct recognition of the nature of mind.

07

Bonds and Relationships+

Couple conflict is rarely about what it seems to be about. Underneath every argument there are two nervous systems trying to find safety. I accompany couples in stepping out of the fighting loop to find what is really being asked for.

I work following the guidelines of Sue Johnson, a pioneer in attachment-based couple therapy.

The process

How we work
in three phases

01

Regulation

Stabilizing the nervous system, recovering ground, normalizing what has arrived after a crisis, an experience, or a difficult life moment. Before looking inward, first one must be able to hold oneself.

02

Elaboration

Giving words, gestures and symbols to what has been lived. Naming what asks to be named. Allowing the body to complete the movements that were left unfinished and emotions to find their form.

03

Anchoring

Translating what has been learned into concrete changes: the way we inhabit the body, our relationships, our work, our time. So that understanding becomes life, not just idea.

Before

About the approach

Are you a psychologist or a psychotherapist?

I am a psychotherapist, and it is worth knowing what that means, because the difference probably works in your favour.

A Psychology degree is the study of the mind in general: statistics, neuroscience, social, organisational and developmental psychology. Very little of that is doing therapy. You can finish the degree without ever having accompanied anyone.

Psychotherapy is another matter: years of training in a specific method, supervised practice and one’s own personal therapy, because nobody can accompany you to a place they have never been themselves. I have been doing this for ten years, with training in Gestalt, NARM somatosensory trauma work and Claudio Naranjo’s SAT programme.

And there is something rarely mentioned: half a century of research comparing therapeutic schools concludes that outcomes depend far more on the relationship and on the particular therapist than on their qualification or their school. I explain this in depth in this article.

When you need a clinical psychologist rather than me: if you are looking for a clinical diagnosis, an expert report, medication or insurance coverage. In that case I will refer you to someone I trust in the first session.

What is integrative humanistic psychotherapy?

It starts from a basic idea: the human being is not a broken system that needs to be fixed, but a developmental process that sometimes gets blocked. The task is not to diagnose and apply a technique, but to create the conditions for that natural process to resume its course. "Integrative" means that I combine different traditions — gestalt, transpersonal, somatosensory — according to what each person needs at each moment.

How is it different from more protocol-based therapies?

Protocol-based therapies (cognitive-behavioral, for example) work with relatively closed schemes: identifying irrational beliefs, dismantling patterns, reprogramming responses. My approach starts from listening and present experience. I don't try to "fix" you — I accompany you to understand what keeps repeating in you and to find your own resources to live with more authenticity.

Do you also work with the body?

Yes. I come from a training in somatosensory body psychotherapy. In session we don't only talk: we pay attention to how you breathe when something emerges, what tenses, what closes, what asks to move. The body holds information that sometimes doesn't reach words — working with it allows integration of experiences that stay stuck when approached only from the rational.

What is transpersonal psychotherapy?

It's a branch of the humanistic tradition that incorporates spiritual dimensions and expanded states of consciousness without pathologizing them. It is especially useful when suffering also has an existential dimension: loss of meaning, life crises, deep self-knowledge processes, integration of non-ordinary experiences.

What topics can you help me with?

The topics I work with most frequently: trauma (especially relational and developmental trauma), anxiety, nervous system self-regulation, bonds and attachment, identity crisis, burnout, depression from a humanistic view, integration of psychedelic experiences, grief processes, highly sensitive people (HSP).

About the sessions

What is a first session like?

The first session is a meeting where you tell me what brings you here, and I listen. There are no questionnaires or formal evaluations. At the end, if you feel the bond can work and I also see it possible, we define a working rhythm. If you need another resource — a psychiatrist, another therapeutic approach —, I'll tell you clearly.

How long does a process last?

It depends on the depth of the work and your pace. Some processes are completed in 8–12 sessions when there is a clear and delimited focus. Others extend over several months or more than a year when there is vital reconstruction, complex trauma, or identity change. I don't work with closed packages — we define the process together and you can stop whenever you feel you have finished.

How often are the sessions?

The most common is once a week. At the beginning of the process or in moments of high intensity, twice a week may make sense; in integration phases, sessions may space out to every two weeks.

Do you also work online?

Yes. Many processes work just as well online as in person. The only practical difference: sessions that include intense body or expressive work are recommended in person.

What languages do you work in?

Spanish, Catalan, English and Romanian.
